Imagine getting a call from someone who sounds like your grandchild. They say they are in trouble, need money urgently, and cannot explain everything. It is natural to feel worried and want to help, but this could be a **grandparent scam**.

This scam is a type of impersonation fraud. A criminal pretends to be a grandchild or another relative and invents an emergency to pressure a family member into sending money. The good news is that a simple pause-and-check routine can help protect everyone.

## Step 1: Understand how the grandparent scam works

The call may begin with a question such as, “Grandma, do you know who this is?” The caller hopes you will say a name, giving them a relative to impersonate.

They may claim that your grandchild has been arrested, had an accident, lost their passport, or needs urgent medical help. Sometimes another person joins the call and pretends to be a lawyer, police officer, or hospital employee.

The caller usually asks for money quickly. They may also insist that you keep the situation secret because the grandchild is embarrassed or does not want their parents to know. That secrecy is part of the pressure.

The scam can arrive by phone, text, email, or social media message. The person contacting you may already know family names and other details from public posts or compromised accounts. That information does not prove the story is genuine.

> **Good to know:** A familiar name, phone number, or voice is not proof of identity. Scammers can manipulate caller ID and may use artificial intelligence (AI) to imitate someone's voice.

## Step 2: Recognize the warning signs

Grandparent scams are designed to make you react before you have time to check the facts. Watch for these common warning signs:

1. **A sudden emergency:** The caller says a loved one is in jail, in hospital, stranded, or in another serious situation.
2. **Pressure to act immediately:** They say money must arrive within minutes or something terrible will happen.
3. **A demand for secrecy:** They tell you not to contact the person's parents or anyone else in the family.
4. **Unusual payment instructions:** They ask for gift cards, cryptocurrency, a wire transfer, cash, or a courier to collect money.
5. **A second caller claiming authority:** Someone says they are a lawyer, police officer, or hospital representative and confirms the story, but gives no independently verifiable details.

Any one of these signs is a reason to stop and verify. Do not let the caller make you feel guilty for taking time to check.

[SCREENSHOT: A sample text message containing urgency and secrecy language, clearly labeled as an illustrative scam example]

> **Tip:** You do not have to decide whether a story is real while you are on the call. It is perfectly reasonable to say, “I need to check this. I will call you back.”

## Step 3: Verify the caller before doing anything

The safest response is to end the conversation and contact your relative using a number you already have saved. Do not call a number supplied by the person who contacted you.

1. Hang up or stop replying to the message.
2. Call the grandchild on their usual phone number.
3. If they do not answer, contact their parent, another relative, or a trusted family friend.
4. If the caller mentioned a hospital, police station, or court, look up that organization's official number yourself and ask whether the claim can be verified.
5. Do not send money or share personal or banking information while you are checking.

If the situation is genuine, a short delay to confirm the facts is still sensible. A real emergency does not require you to follow secret payment instructions from an unknown caller.

[SCREENSHOT: A phone contact list showing a saved family contact being called to verify an unexpected request]

> **Careful:** Do not rely on a voice sounding exactly like your grandchild. AI voice-cloning tools can imitate a person's voice from audio found online. Always verify through a separate, trusted contact method.

## Step 4: Agree on a family safety plan

A family plan makes it easier to respond calmly if someone receives a frightening call. Discuss it before an emergency happens, and make sure everyone knows they can check a request without being judged.

1. **Choose a family code word or phrase.** Agree on something that is not shared publicly and can be used to confirm identity in a genuine emergency.
2. **Keep contact details up to date.** Save relatives' usual phone numbers and an additional trusted contact.
3. **Agree that money requests must be verified.** No one should send money based only on an unexpected call, text, or voice message.
4. **Make it acceptable to call another family member.** Nobody should feel obliged to keep an emergency request secret.
5. **Share less personal information publicly.** Review social media privacy settings and avoid posting details such as travel plans, addresses, or personal contact information.

[SCREENSHOT: A family contact sheet with saved phone numbers and a reminder to verify urgent money requests]

The code word should be kept private. Do not send it in a group chat or post it online, and change it if you think someone outside the family has learned it.

## Step 5: Know what to do if money has already been sent

If you or someone in your family has paid a suspected scammer, act quickly. There is no guarantee the money can be recovered, but contacting the payment provider immediately may help.

1. Contact the bank, card issuer, money transfer service, or payment app using its official number or app. Explain that the payment may be fraudulent and ask whether it can be stopped or reversed.
2. If gift card details were shared, contact the gift card company and keep the card and receipt.
3. Save messages, phone numbers, emails, receipts, and payment records. Do not delete evidence.
4. Report the incident to the appropriate fraud-reporting service and local police if money was lost or threats were made.
5. Tell a trusted family member. The person targeted deserves support, not blame.

In the US, report the scam to the Federal Trade Commission at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/). You can also contact the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at [IC3.gov](https://www.ic3.gov/) for an online report.

In the UK, report fraud to [Report Fraud](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk/), the national service that replaced Action Fraud in December 2025. If someone is in immediate danger, contact emergency services.

> **Good to know:** Contact the bank or payment provider even if the transfer happened a little while ago. The sooner you report it, the sooner they can explain what options may still be available.

## Troubleshooting: Common questions about grandparent scams

**What if the caller knows my grandchild's name?** Names and family details can sometimes be found online or through exposed accounts. Treat that information as a clue, not proof. Verify by calling your relative using a number you already trust.

**What if the voice sounds exactly right?** Do not use the voice as your only test. AI can imitate voices. End the call and use a saved number or another family member to confirm the situation.

**What if the caller says not to tell anyone?** That is a major warning sign. A genuine emergency does not prevent you from checking with another trusted person.

**Should I answer calls from unknown numbers?** You can let an unfamiliar call go to voicemail. If the caller claims to be a relative in trouble, contact that person directly rather than relying on the incoming call.

## Protect your family with one simple rule

The grandparent scam works by creating fear and urgency, but you do not have to respond immediately. Pause, end the conversation, and verify the story using a trusted number before sharing information or sending money. Make that your family's rule, and remind one another that checking first is always the right thing to do.
